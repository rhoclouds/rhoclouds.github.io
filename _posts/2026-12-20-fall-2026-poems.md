In fall 2025, I tried to write a poem a day for 21 days. Nearly all of them were free verse. This fall, I'm challenging myself to write a poem every day of the season, each following a defined meter. My goals are to see if the poetry is better overall and to see the rate at which my writing improves over the course of each challenge. I unfortunately missed some days. My conclusion and an Appendix serving as a brief guide to poetic meter and form can be found at the end.

# Appendix

## A.1. Fundamentals of Poetic Meter

  Meter is the recurring pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line of poetry. In English verse, it is determined by both the number of syllables and the arrangement of stresses. A metrical foot is the basic rhythmic unit of a line. Traditional English poetry employs several principal types of feet.

| Foot | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Iamb | ˘ / | a**BOVE** |
| Trochee | / ˘ | **GAR**den |
| Anapest | ˘ ˘ / | under**STAND** |
| Dactyl | / ˘ ˘ | **BEAU**tiful |
| Amphibrach | ˘ / ˘ | re**MEM**ber |

Here, `˘` denotes an unstressed syllable and `/` denotes a stressed syllable. A spondee contains two stresses, while a pyrrhic foot contains two unstressed syllables

The number of feet determines the metrical length of a line.

| Feet | Term |
|---|---|
| 1 | Monometer |
| 2 | Dimeter |
| 3 | Trimeter |
| 4 | Tetrameter |
| 5 | Pentameter |
| 6 | Hexameter |
| 7 | Heptameter |
| 8 | Octameter |

For example, *iambic pentameter* consists of five iambic feet, normally producing ten syllables.

```text
˘  /  | ˘  /  | ˘  /  | ˘  /  | ˘  /
```


## A.2. The Five Principal Meters

### Iambic Meter

An **iamb** consists of an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable.

**Pattern:** ˘ /

Example from William Shakespeare's *Sonnet 18* (1609), line 13:

> So long as men can breathe or eyes can see,

```text
so LONG | as MEN | can BREATHE | or EYES | can SEE
```

This is iambic pentameter, containing five iambic feet.

Iambic meter is especially common in English dramatic verse, sonnets, and narrative poetry.

### Trochaic Meter

A **trochee** reverses the iamb, beginning with a stressed syllable followed by an unstressed syllable.

**Pattern:** / ˘

Example from William Blake's *The Tyger* (1794), line 1:

> Tyger Tyger, burning bright,

```text
TYger | TYger | BURNing | BRIGHT
```

This illustrates trochaic tetrameter with a catalectic, or shortened, final foot.

Trochaic verse often creates a forceful, descending rhythm. Trochaic tetrameter contains four trochaic feet, while trochaic pentameter contains five.

### Anapestic Meter

An **anapest** contains two unstressed syllables followed by one stressed syllable.

**Pattern:** ˘ ˘ /

Example from Lord Byron's *The Destruction of Sennacherib* (1815), line 3:

> And the sheen of their spears was like stars on the sea,

```text
and the SHEEN | of their SPEARS | was like STARS | on the SEA
```

This illustrates anapestic tetrameter, with four three-syllable feet.

Anapestic verse often produces a rolling, forward-moving rhythm, making it useful for narrative movement, anticipation, and musicality.

### Dactylic Meter

A **dactyl** contains one stressed syllable followed by two unstressed syllables.

**Pattern:** / ˘ ˘

Example from Alfred, Lord Tennyson's *The Charge of the Light Brigade* (1854), line 1:

> Half a league, half a league,

```text
HALF a league | HALF a league
```

This illustrates dactylic dimeter, containing two dactylic feet.

Dactylic tetrameter contains four dactylic feet, ordinarily twelve syllables when every foot is complete.

Dactylic verse has a falling rhythm, frequently associated with classical epic traditions, although classical quantitative meter differs from English stress-based meter.

### Amphibrachic Meter

An **amphibrach** contains an unstressed syllable, a stressed syllable, and another unstressed syllable.

**Pattern:** ˘ / ˘

Example from Samuel Woodworth's *The Old Oaken Bucket* (1817), line 1:

> How dear to my heart are the scenes of my childhood,

```text
how DEAR to | my HEART are | the SCENES of | my CHILDhood
```

This illustrates amphibrachic tetrameter, with four three-syllable feet.

Its alternating rise and fall creates a fluid rhythm, well suited to lyrical and reflective poetry.

## A.3. Traditional Forms

### Common Meter

**Common meter** alternates iambic tetrameter and iambic trimeter, producing a syllabic pattern of 8–6–8–6.

Rhyme scheme is ABCB, although ABAB also occurs.

Common meter is frequently associated with English hymns, ballads, and narrative poetry.

### Shakespearean Sonnet

A **Shakespearean sonnet** contains fourteen lines, ordinarily written in iambic pentameter.

**Rhyme scheme:** ABAB CDCD EFEF GG

It consists of three quatrains followed by a closing couplet.

The couplet frequently delivers a resolution, reversal, or concentrated statement of the poem's central idea.

The transition in argument or perspective is called the *volta*.

### Petrarchan Sonnet

A **Petrarchan sonnet** also contains fourteen lines, typically in iambic pentameter when written in English.

Its structure consists of an eight-line **octave** and a six-line **sestet**.

A traditional rhyme scheme is:

**ABBA ABBA CDE CDE**

Other sestet patterns are possible, including CDC DCD.

The volta commonly occurs between the octave and sestet, where the poem moves from a situation or problem toward reflection, response, or resolution.

### Sapphic Stanza

The classical **Sapphic stanza** consists of three Sapphic hendecasyllabic lines followed by a shorter Adonic line.

In English adaptations, poets frequently approximate the original quantitative meter using stress and syllable patterns.

The challenge's *Refraction* uses a simplified English Sapphic structure, with three longer lines followed by a short concluding line.

A fixed end-rhyme scheme is not required.

### Alliterative Verse

**Alliterative verse** organizes its rhythm primarily through stressed beats and the repetition of initial consonant sounds.

Traditional Old English alliterative verse generally contains four principal stresses per line, divided into two half-lines by a caesura.

Alliteration connects stressed words across the half-lines. Unlike accentual-syllabic meter, the number of unstressed syllables is comparatively flexible.

The challenge's *Filaments* uses a simplified four-beat alliterative structure.

### Terza Rima

**Terza rima** is a form of interlocking tercets traditionally associated with Dante's *Divine Comedy*.

Its rhyme scheme proceeds by carrying the middle rhyme of each tercet into the outer lines of the next.

For the fourteen-line adaptation used in *Reprise*:

**ABA BCB CDC DED EE**

The poem consists of four tercets and a closing couplet.

| Stanza | Rhyme scheme | *Reprise* rhyme words |
|---|---|---|
| First tercet | ABA | spice / gold / ice |
| Second tercet | BCB | cold / blow / hold |
| Third tercet | CDC | snow / you / know |
| Fourth tercet | DED | through / ember / blue |
| Closing couplet | EE | remember / December |

Terza rima creates continuity between stanzas because each tercet introduces a rhyme that the following tercet must resolve.

The closing couplet completes the final rhyme while providing a natural point of conclusion.
